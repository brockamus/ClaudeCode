# Why Clean-Beauty Founders Are Skipping Retinol (And What They're Using Instead)

For years, retinol was the undisputed workhorse of evidence-based skincare. The clinical literature on it is substantial — photoaging, acne, hyperpigmentation — and no serious formulator dismissed it. But I've watched a quiet shift happen over the past 18 months, and I think it matters more than most brand-side commentary has acknowledged yet.

Retinol is increasingly complicated to sell.

The problem isn't efficacy. It's that a meaningful and growing segment of the beauty market — pregnant women, breastfeeding women, people with sensitized or compromised skin barriers — has been told, correctly, to avoid it. The teratogenicity concern is real. The guidance from OBs is consistent: stop retinoids when you're trying to conceive, during pregnancy, while breastfeeding. That's potentially 3–5 years of a customer's life during which she can't use your hero active.

For brands selling to the 28–40 demographic — which is most of clean beauty — that's not a niche edge case. That's a significant portion of the TAM being told by their doctors to put your product down.

## The gap this creates

I started hearing about this from our own customers in 2023. Women who had been on skincare journeys for years, who cared deeply about actives and results, who were now pregnant or postpartum and asking: what actually works that I can still use? They weren't interested in a retinol alternative that was just a gentler retinol. They wanted something with a different mechanism entirely — something with clinical backing, not just "gentle enough to use during pregnancy" marketing language.

That led me into a research sprint that most clean-beauty founders in my position probably recognize. You read everything. You look at the clinical literature. You talk to formulators. You end up somewhere unexpected.

For us, that somewhere was PDRN — polydeoxyribonucleotide.

## Why PDRN specifically

PDRN works via a completely different pathway than retinol. Rather than accelerating cell turnover (which is retinol's primary mechanism, and the reason for the purge, the sensitivity, the mandatory SPF escalation), PDRN binds to A2A adenosine receptors and triggers the body's own repair signaling. The clinical results that exist — and they're mostly from injectable applications in dermatology, which is worth acknowledging — show collagen synthesis, wound healing, anti-inflammatory activity.

The topical application is newer. The data is thinner. I'll be direct about that, because I think founders who oversell ingredient science destroy their own credibility over time. What I can say is that the mechanism is plausible, the ingredient is genuine, and the patient-safety profile during pregnancy hasn't raised the kind of flags that retinol has. That last point alone made it worth investigating seriously.

The bigger supply chain issue, which I don't see founders talking about enough, is sourcing. The dominant industrial source for PDRN is salmon-derived — specifically from salmon testes, a byproduct of aquaculture. The ingredient is well-established in pharmaceutical-grade injectable products out of Italy and Korea. But for a clean-beauty brand positioning around veganism, transparency, and supply-chain integrity, that's a problem.

Vegan-certified PDRN exists, but it's rare, more expensive, and requires actual supplier-level diligence to verify. Plant-derived and biotech-fermented alternatives are available, but the documentation infrastructure is underdeveloped. You have to go deep to confirm what you're getting.

## What we did, and what we learned

We spent about eight months in formulation before we were confident in our sourcing. We vetted three suppliers. Two couldn't provide documentation I was satisfied with at the enzyme or extraction stage. One was using what turned out to be an ambiguous "plant-derived" claim that fell apart when I asked for the spec sheet.

The supplier we ultimately worked with provided full manufacturing process documentation, stability data, and was willing to answer follow-up questions about their fermentation methodology. That level of access is what I'd recommend any founder look for when evaluating an unfamiliar active — not just the certificate of analysis, but the process behind it.

We built our serum around non-salmon-derived PDRN, and it's become the most-requested product in our line. You can see how we approached the formulation at [our serum collection page](https://leafandbird.com/collections/pdrn-serum), which walks through what we made and why. The commercial result has been meaningful, but the more interesting outcome was what the formulation process taught us about supply chain vetting that we've now applied to other actives.

## The broader pattern

PDRN is one example, but the underlying dynamic isn't specific to this ingredient. The clean-beauty customer in 2026 is more sophisticated about actives than she was five years ago. She reads INCI lists. She follows dermatologists on social media. She knows the difference between a peptide and a retinoid. She's also, in many cases, navigating pregnancy, postpartum, or a sensitized skin barrier, and she's actively looking for efficacious alternatives that don't require her to accept "this won't irritate you" as the sole value proposition.

Founders who are still centering their formulation strategy around retinol — even clean retinol, even encapsulated retinol — are going to find themselves on the wrong side of that conversation for a growing portion of their audience.

The actives pipeline that makes more sense to me right now, for this customer: PDRN, bakuchiol (not as a retinol alternative, but as its own thing with its own clinical profile), niacinamide formulated at concentrations that actually do something, and some of the emerging AHA/enzyme hybrid approaches that resurface without the irritation ceiling.

None of these are silver bullets. But they're where I'd be spending formulation budget if I were starting a brand today.

The retinol era isn't over. But the clean-beauty founder who's only asking "how do we do retinol better" is missing a more interesting question: what do we build for the customer retinol can't serve?

That's the market that's wide open right now.
