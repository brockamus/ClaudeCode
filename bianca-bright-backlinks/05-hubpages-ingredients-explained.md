# Teeth Whitening Ingredients Explained: What to Look For and What to Avoid

The teeth whitening market is projected to exceed $11 billion globally by 2028, and the ingredient lists on these products range from well-researched clinical compounds to questionable fillers that have no business being in your mouth. Understanding what is actually in your whitening product — and what those ingredients do — is the single best way to get results without damaging your teeth.

This guide breaks down the most common whitening ingredients, explains the science behind each one, and identifies the substances worth avoiding.

## The Active Whitening Agents

**Hydrogen Peroxide**

Hydrogen peroxide is the most widely used whitening agent in dentistry. It works through an oxidation reaction — the peroxide breaks down into water and free oxygen radicals, which penetrate the enamel and break apart the chromogen compounds (stain molecules) embedded in the tooth structure.

Concentration matters significantly. Professional in-office treatments may use 25 to 40 percent hydrogen peroxide under controlled conditions. Over-the-counter products typically range from 3 to 10 percent. The American Dental Association considers concentrations up to 10 percent safe for home use when used as directed. Higher concentrations increase the risk of enamel demineralization, gum irritation, and tooth sensitivity.

**Carbamide Peroxide**

Carbamide peroxide is essentially a slow-release form of hydrogen peroxide. A 10 percent carbamide peroxide gel breaks down to roughly 3.5 percent hydrogen peroxide over time. This slower release makes it gentler and is why many overnight whitening trays use carbamide peroxide rather than hydrogen peroxide directly.

**PAP (Phthalimidoperoxycaproic Acid)**

PAP is a newer whitening agent that has gained popularity in the last several years, particularly in brands like Hismile. Unlike peroxide, PAP whitens through a non-radical oxidation process, meaning it does not generate free radicals that can potentially irritate living tissue. Early research is promising — a 2020 study published in Clinical Oral Investigations found PAP effective at whitening without measurable enamel damage.

## The Support Ingredients

**Hydroxyapatite**

Hydroxyapatite is a naturally occurring mineral that makes up approximately 97 percent of tooth enamel. In whitening products, it serves a remineralization function — it bonds to the tooth surface and fills microscopic defects in the enamel, which can reduce sensitivity and may improve the appearance of teeth by creating a smoother, more light-reflective surface.

Originally developed for dental applications in Japan (where it has been an approved anti-cavity agent since 1993), hydroxyapatite has gained global traction in oral care. Brands focused on [enamel-safe whitening](https://biancabright.com/collections/enamel-safe-teeth-whitening) increasingly include it as a core ingredient.

**Potassium Nitrate**

Potassium nitrate is a well-established desensitizing agent found in products like Sensodyne. It works by calming the nerve inside the tooth — potassium ions penetrate the dentinal tubules and reduce the nerve's ability to transmit pain signals. In whitening products, potassium nitrate serves a preventive role, addressing sensitivity during the whitening process rather than after the fact.

**Sodium Bicarbonate (Baking Soda)**

Baking soda is a mild abrasive that can remove surface stains through mechanical action. It is safe at low concentrations and has a long history of use in oral care. However, it does not penetrate enamel and cannot address intrinsic (below-surface) staining.

## What to Avoid

**Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS)**

SLS is a foaming agent found in many toothpastes and some whitening products. While it is not directly harmful to enamel, it is a known irritant for soft tissue. Research published in the Journal of Clinical Periodontology has linked SLS-containing toothpastes to increased frequency of canker sores in susceptible individuals. Brands committed to [natural teeth whitening](https://biancabright.com/collections/natural-teeth-whitening) formulations typically exclude it.

**Alcohol-Based Carriers**

Some whitening gels and rinses use alcohol as a solvent or carrier agent. Alcohol dries out oral tissue, reduces saliva production, and can exacerbate gum irritation — particularly during active whitening when the tissue may already be sensitized. There is no whitening benefit to alcohol; it is a formulation convenience, not a performance ingredient.

**Artificial Dyes**

There is no functional reason for a whitening product to contain artificial colorants. Blue covarine (found in some "instant white" toothpastes) creates a temporary optical illusion of whiter teeth by depositing a blue film, but it does not change the actual shade.

**Extremely High Peroxide Concentrations**

Over-the-counter products with hydrogen peroxide above 10 percent should be approached with caution. At these concentrations, the risk of chemical burns to gum tissue increases substantially.

## What a Clean Whitening Formula Looks Like

Based on current research, an ideal whitening product would include a proven whitening agent (hydrogen peroxide, carbamide peroxide, or PAP) at a safe and effective concentration, hydroxyapatite for enamel remineralization, and potassium nitrate for sensitivity prevention. It would exclude SLS, alcohol, artificial dyes, and parabens.

The simplest rule: if you cannot identify why an ingredient is in the formula, it probably should not be.
