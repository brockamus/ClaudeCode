# I Stopped Making My Own Tallow Face Cream. Here's Why.

The last batch I made myself smelled like a pot roast cooling on the stove.

Not faintly. Not in a way I could talk myself out of. I was standing at my kitchen counter at 9:30 on a Wednesday night, the stand mixer going, the rendered fat still a little too warm, and my house genuinely smelled like Sunday dinner. My husband walked in and looked at the mixing bowl, then at me, then back at the bowl, and said: "Is that... your face moisturizer?"

It was.

I'd been making tallow face cream for almost two years at that point. I had a rhythm. I had a supplier for the suet. I had a stash of essential oils in the cabinet above the stove and a rotation of botanicals I was pretty confident in. I'd made maybe thirty batches, given jars to friends, kept a running notes document about what worked and what didn't. I was proud of it.

And then I wasn't anymore.

---

Let me back up to where this started.

I had a reaction to a conventional moisturizer when I was about four months postpartum with my first daughter. Nothing dramatic — just a slow, creeping redness across my cheeks that started three weeks after I switched products and took me another three weeks to connect to the moisturizer. I'd used it for two years before getting pregnant without any issues. Something had shifted.

My midwife, who I trusted completely, mentioned that postpartum skin can be reactive in ways that feel inexplicable because the hormonal withdrawal from pregnancy is abrupt and real. She also mentioned, very casually, that she'd been using tallow for her face for years. "Ancestral skincare," she called it, like she was slightly embarrassed to say it out loud. "Your great-grandmother probably used lard."

I went home and did what I always do when someone says something that surprises me: I read everything I could find. What I found was that tallow — specifically grass-fed beef tallow — has a fatty acid profile that's remarkably close to human sebum. Oleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, conjugated linoleic acid. It absorbs without clogging pores in the way that heavier plant oils sometimes do. It has been used as a skin protectant for centuries in cultures across several continents. The evidence base is mostly historical and anecdotal, but it's not nothing, and the ingredient list is short: beef fat.

I was hooked on the simplicity of it.

---

The first step was finding grass-fed suet. This took longer than I expected. Not because it doesn't exist, but because finding a source I trusted — that could tell me the animal's diet, that wasn't just slapping "grass-fed" on a label — required some work. I eventually found a local farm about 45 minutes away that raised their own beef, finished on grass, and would sell me suet directly. The first time I drove out to pick it up, I brought my daughter in her car seat and thought: this is exactly the kind of thing my grandmother would have done without thinking twice, and I'm treating it like a pilgrimage.

The first render was a revelation. I used a slow cooker on low for about eight hours, strained it twice through cheesecloth, and ended up with the most beautiful ivory fat I had ever seen in my kitchen. It smelled clean — faintly beefy, but not unpleasant. When it solidified it had a slight yellow cast from the beta-carotene in the grass diet. I felt absurdly satisfied.

The first batch of whipped cream worked. I added some jojoba oil to soften it, a little vitamin E, lavender essential oil, and about thirty seconds on high with the stand mixer. It went on smooth. My skin was happy. I gave half the jar to my neighbor, who texted me the next morning that it was the best moisturizer she'd ever used.

This is the part of the story that makes it harder to explain what came next.

---

The batches that didn't work, I have catalogued in that notes document I mentioned.

Batch 7: smelled rancid within three weeks. I had rendered at too high a temperature and not strained carefully enough — some tissue proteins made it through the cheesecloth and accelerated oxidation. I threw it out.

Batch 11: whipped beautifully but separated into a greasy puddle within 48 hours. Temperature when I started whipping was too high. The fat hadn't cooled evenly because I'd rushed it into the mixer.

Batch 14: this one I used for almost two months before I noticed a faint off-smell developing. Nothing alarming, but something had started. My estimate is that I'd contaminated the jar through repeated finger-dipping, which introduced enough moisture to start something I couldn't see.

Batch 19: I tried incorporating a botanical infusion — chamomile flowers steeped in the jojoba oil — and the water content from the infusion was enough to encourage something I didn't want in an anhydrous product. I used it anyway. I shouldn't have.

The successes were real. But the failures were also real, and I started to notice that I couldn't fully predict which way a batch would go. My notes were getting longer and more anxious.

---

The kitchen-sanitation question arrived quietly and stayed.

I don't have a sterile workspace. I have a kitchen. A kitchen where I cook dinner, where my kids make quesadillas with supervision, where there are surfaces that I wipe down but don't sanitize in any meaningful sense. When I was being honest with myself, I couldn't fully answer the question of whether my "clean" batches were actually clean, or just clean enough that nothing had gone wrong yet.

Professional cosmetic manufacturers operate in ISO-certified clean rooms or pharmaceutical-grade facilities. They run challenge testing, which means they deliberately introduce microbial contaminants into a small sample and measure how effectively the preservative system neutralizes them. They validate shelf life with actual accelerated stability testing — weeks in a heat chamber, not hope and smell-checking.

I was doing none of that.

More unsettling: tallow is genuinely antimicrobial by nature, and an anhydrous product (no water) has much lower microbial risk than a lotion or cream. So I wasn't necessarily poisoning myself. But I also couldn't prove I wasn't. And I was giving this to my kids sometimes. I was giving it to my neighbor.

That started to weigh on me in a way it hadn't before.

---

The pivot moment was actually quiet. Not a crisis batch, not a dramatic contamination event. It was a conversation with a woman at a farmer's market who made her own lotion and handed me a jar with so much confidence, and I thought: I used to have that confidence, and somewhere in the notes document I lost it.

I decided I wanted to make something I could stand behind with the same certainty. That meant working with a formulator who understood the ancestral-product category but also had the equipment and testing infrastructure to produce something consistent. It meant finding a rendering source I could verify. It meant building something that could sit on a shelf for 12 months and be exactly what it was on day one.

What I learned in that process that surprised me: the rendering temperature matters more than I'd realized. Professional-grade tallow cream requires the fat to be rendered to a precise temperature and strained through industrial-grade filtration to remove the cellular debris that causes rancidity. My cheesecloth method worked most of the time. Most of the time isn't a standard.

I also learned that botanical sourcing at commercial scale is its own research project. Which lavender, from where, at what concentration, with what third-party quality verification. My essential oils from the cabinet above the stove were fine for personal use. They were harder to justify as an ingredient in something I was selling or giving away.

What I kept from two years of DIY: the grass-fed commitment, the minimal ingredient list, the botanical scents I'd come to love, the philosophy that your moisturizer doesn't need a 30-item INCI list to work.

---

The result, after about a year of working toward it, is [three whipped grass-fed tallow creams](https://leafandbird.com/collections/tallow-cream) — lavender, chamomile, and unscented — that are what I was trying to make in my kitchen, formulated to a standard I couldn't hit at home.

I'm not going to pretend I don't miss the render days entirely. There was something genuinely satisfying about making it myself, about knowing exactly where every ingredient came from because I'd touched every step. That feeling is real. I understand why people keep doing it.

But I also know what's in my notes document. I know batch 7 and batch 14. I know the nights I was smelling jars too carefully, trying to decide whether something was off or whether I was just anxious.

This is not me telling you that DIY is dangerous or wrong. People have been making tallow preparations at home for generations and the bar for safety is genuinely lower when you're making something for yourself and using it fresh. If you render a small batch, store it in a clean glass jar in the fridge, and use it within 30 days, you're probably fine.

I'm saying: if you want the consistency of something that tastes the same every time you open it, if you're giving it to your kids or your friends, if you want a 12-month shelf life and a product that whips identically batch after batch — that's harder than it looks from the outside.

I made it look easy for longer than I should have.
