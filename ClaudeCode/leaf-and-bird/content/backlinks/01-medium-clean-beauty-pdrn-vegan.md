# The Clean-Beauty Question Nobody's Answering: Is PDRN Vegan?

I was deep in a TikTok rabbit hole at 11 PM — the kind of scroll that starts with one video about glass skin and somehow lands you forty minutes later watching a Korean dermatologist explain why salmon DNA is having a moment in skincare. That's when I first heard the letters: P-D-R-N. Polydeoxyribonucleotide. The algorithm had decided I needed to know about it, and once you see the before-and-afters, it's hard to unsee them.

The claims were aggressive in the way that K-beauty claims often are — not in a misleading way, but in the "we've been studying this for thirty years and the West just found out" way. Wound healing. Cellular repair. The kind of deep skin regeneration that retinol tries to approximate by irritating your way to new cells. PDRN supposedly worked differently: by binding to receptors that signal tissue repair at the DNA level, triggering the body's own healing cascade rather than forcing exfoliation.

I took notes. I went searching for a serum.

And then I read the full ingredient story.

## What PDRN actually is

Polydeoxyribonucleotide is a biopolymer extracted from the DNA of living cells — specifically, from the DNA strands that make up the double helix. When those strands are broken down into shorter nucleotide sequences and purified, what you're left with is a bioactive molecule that can interact with a specific receptor in human tissue: the A2A adenosine receptor.

Activating that receptor does a few meaningful things. It triggers growth factor production (VEGF, TGF-β) that signals skin cells to repair and regenerate. It has documented anti-inflammatory effects. It appears to increase collagen synthesis. In the clinical literature — and there is genuine clinical literature, mostly from Italian and Korean dermatology journals — injectable PDRN has been used for wound healing after surgery, for skin grafts, for hair loss treatment.

The topical application is newer and less studied, but the mechanism makes theoretical sense: smaller nucleotide fragments can penetrate the stratum corneum, at least partially. Whether the efficacy of injectable PDRN fully translates to topical is still an open question, and anyone telling you otherwise is overselling. What's clear is that the ingredient has real biological activity. It's not a marketing fiction.

The DNA source, though — that's where it gets complicated for anyone paying attention to where their ingredients come from.

## The salmon thing

The dominant industrial source for PDRN is salmon. Specifically, salmon sperm. The testes of farmed salmon are a byproduct of the fish processing industry in Norway and other salmon-farming nations, and they happen to be extraordinarily rich in DNA. Extracting PDRN from salmon sperm is efficient, cheap, and has been refined over decades. The pharmaceutical PDRN that powers the clinical literature — Rejuvenyl, Placentex Integro — is salmon-derived.

This isn't hidden exactly, but it's also not prominently disclosed on most K-beauty product labels. If you're reading the INCI list — and this is the teachable moment I promised myself I'd include — the ingredient will appear as **PDRN**, **Polydeoxyribonucleotide**, or sometimes **Salmon DNA Extract** or **Salmon Egg Extract**. That last one is reasonably transparent. The first two are not. You cannot tell from the INCI name "Polydeoxyribonucleotide" alone whether the source was salmon, plant cells, or a fermentation tank in a biotech lab.

The way to know is to look for a certification (Vegan Society or PETA-approved) or to contact the brand directly and ask for their PDRN supplier's spec sheet. Some will provide it. Many won't. In an industry where "natural" has no legal definition and "clean" is entirely self-assigned, supply chain opacity is the norm, not the exception.

I learned this the hard way.

## The ick factor, the ethics factor, the supply-chain factor

These are three separate things, and I kept confusing them early in my research.

The *ick factor* is just the visceral reaction to the phrase "salmon sperm." It doesn't mean the ingredient is dangerous or ineffective. It means it sounds gross to people who weren't expecting it. This is a real thing that shapes purchasing decisions, but it's not a moral argument.

The *ethics factor* is whether using animal-derived ingredients — specifically reproductive byproducts from farmed fish — aligns with a vegan framework. For me, and for the Leaf & Bird customer I was building for, the answer was no. Farmed salmon come with significant environmental concerns (sea lice, escapement into wild populations, water quality), and the ingredient is an animal byproduct in the most direct sense. That's a genuine ethical line, and I wasn't willing to cross it.

The *supply-chain factor* is subtler: even if you were neutral on the vegan question, sourcing an ingredient whose quality depends on freshness and cold-chain handling from aquaculture byproduct streams in another country involves real consistency risks. The pharmaceutical-grade injectable PDRN has quality controls. The PDRN in a $28 serum at a major retailer? I genuinely don't know where it came from or how it was handled.

This is not me dunking on competitors. I don't know who's sourcing well and who isn't. My point is that the information infrastructure doesn't exist yet for a shopper to answer that question, which is a problem.

## What vegan PDRN actually means

The short answer: it means the nucleotide sequences come from a non-animal source. The practical options in 2024–2025 are plant-derived DNA (from fruit cells, most commonly), biotech-synthesized oligonucleotides produced through fermentation, or synthetic analogues that mimic PDRN activity without being biological DNA at all.

These are not all equivalent. A plant-sourced PDRN may have different nucleotide composition than salmon-derived PDRN, which could affect how it interacts with A2A receptors. The biotech-fermented route is closer in molecular structure to the pharmaceutical standard but tends to cost significantly more and requires more rigorous supplier vetting. Synthetic analogues sidestep the sourcing question entirely but are also furthest from the clinical research base.

When I started formulating, I spent about eight months trying to find a supplier whose vegan PDRN I trusted enough to put in a product with our name on it. That meant asking for certificates of analysis, reading stability data, and in several cases, going back to the source and asking: show me the manufacturing process. Two suppliers I was initially excited about couldn't provide documentation I was satisfied with. One was using a plant extraction method I couldn't verify was truly animal-free at the enzyme stage. It took longer than I expected to find a source I could stand behind.

That process is what eventually led to [our flagship vegan PDRN serum](https://leafandbird.com/blogs/journal/is-pdrn-vegan) — and more specifically to a 3,500-word piece on our journal that goes deep into the sourcing question, the science, and what we actually ended up using. I wrote it as the document I wished had existed when I was starting out.

## What this means for you, the reader

You don't have to care about any of this. PDRN is genuinely promising as an active, and the salmon-derived version used in clinical research works. If you're not vegan and you're not thinking about aquaculture supply chains when you buy skincare, you have plenty of good options.

But if you are in the camp of readers who reads ingredient lists on their food *and* their skincare, and you've been hearing about PDRN and wondering whether it fits your framework — the answer is: it depends, and you have to ask.

Ask the brand directly: is your PDRN salmon-derived? Watch for "Salmon DNA Extract" or "Salmon Egg Extract" in the INCI. Look for third-party vegan certifications. If a brand won't tell you their source, that tells you something.

The K-beauty industry has produced some of the most rigorous cosmetic science of the last decade, and PDRN is a real candidate for the next major active. The sourcing story hasn't caught up to the hype yet. But it will — and the brands that have already figured out a transparent supply chain will be ahead of that conversation when it does.

I started this because an algorithm showed me a TikTok. I kept going because the question turned out to be genuinely hard, and genuinely worth answering. I hope this saved you some of that research time.
